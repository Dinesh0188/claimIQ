Catch the deduction before the insurer does.

You submit the packet. Three weeks later the money comes back short, with a deduction sheet nobody on the desk fully understands. The patient has gone home, the bill is closed, and the difference is a write-off or a fight.

ClaimIQ reads the packet before it goes to the TPA and splits the bill three ways — so the argument happens while you can still do something about it.

What the insurer pays

What actually settles once the room limit, sub-limits and co-pay are applied — shown as a range, because insurers read the proportionate-deduction rule differently and pretending otherwise would be a guess with a decimal point.

What the patient owes

Optional items and policy deductions, knowable at admission instead of at the discharge counter. The room-rent trap is the big one: a room over the cap scales down the surgeon, OT and nursing charges too.

What you are writing off

Charges already covered by the room or procedure rate. Billed anyway, paid by nobody, and repeating on every claim until the billing master is corrected. This is the number a blocklist cannot show you.

And the arithmetic behind the biggest number

₹97,685 Room rent proportionate

Associated charges scaled by the ratio the room exceeded its cap, under the ‘typical’ insurer profile.

associated charges Rs 195,370.00 heads included CONSULTATION, NURSING, OTHER, PROCEDURE heads excluded IMPLANT, INVESTIGATION, PHARMACY, ROOM eligible proportion 6,000 / 12,000 = 0.5000 195,370.00 x (1 - 0.5000) = 97,685.00

Amounts are exact to the paisa and rounded to the nearest rupee for display, halves up. The model never does arithmetic — a deterministic calculator does, and a verifier rejects any explanation whose figures the calculator did not produce.

Bill, discharge summary, claim form, policy schedule, reports. Native PDFs are parsed exactly; scans and phone photos are read with on-device OCR. The room rate, the dates and the diagnosis are read out of your documents rather than retyped — and anything a document does not state, ClaimIQ asks for instead of assuming.

See the split before you submit

Estimated settlement, patient liability and hospital write-off, with a range across three insurer interpretations. Every deduction is expandable to the rule, its citation and the arithmetic, so you can check the number rather than trust it.

Stop the leak at source

List I items are the patient's cost — tell them at admission. Lists II, III and IV are a billing-template error that repeats on every claim carrying those lines. The dashboard ranks them by aggregate loss across your whole book, so you fix the expensive ones first.

Before you ask procurement

Where the rules come from, and where your data goes.

This is the section most tools leave vague. Straight answers, including the uncomfortable ones.

Where the rule data comes from

Every non-payable determination cites a rule in an inspectable corpus, and every rule names the document it derives from:

  • Lists I–IV correspond to Annexures I–IV of the IRDAI Guidelines on Standardization in Health Insurance (IRDA/HLT/REG/CIR/146/07/2016), with Annexure I as revised by IRDAI/HLT/REG/CIR/176/09/2019.
  • Policy wording used for the deduction mechanics is synthetic and labelled as such — it illustrates how a room cap works; it is not any insurer's contract.

How current it is — honestly

The corpus is a snapshot, and the product says so on every audit rather than burying it:

  • Each source carries a published date, a retrieved date and a verification status. Nothing is currently marked verified, so every audit shows a standing notice to that effect.
  • Whether the 2024 IRDAI Master Circular supersedes the 2016/2019 guidelines has not been confirmed, and the product does not claim it either way.
  • Rules that cannot name their source do not load. The corpus fails closed rather than serving an unattributable determination.

PHI and where it travels

  • Runs locally, bound to 127.0.0.1. Documents are parsed on your machine and the database is a local file.
  • OCR is on-device. A native PDF with a text layer is parsed with no model call at all.
  • Scans and photos need a language model to structure the OCR text. If you configure a provider, that text — which contains clinical detail — is sent to it. That is stated plainly because it is the question a compliance officer will ask.
  • There is no authentication and no multi-tenancy. It is a single-user local tool. Running it against real claims in a shared environment would need auth, tenancy and audit logging first.

What it is not

  • Not an adjudicator. It estimates likely deductions under configurable rules; a human decides.
  • Not a claims-management system. The audit is stateless — it does not submit or track anything.
  • Not a source of legal truth, and it says so on screen.
Measured, not claimed. With no model configured, the offline classifier identifies 74.6% of genuinely non-payable items and wrongly disallows 0.0% of genuine medical charges, measured against 87 hand-labelled line items (scripts/bench_classify.py). Roughly one non-payable item in four is missed in that mode, and the product states this on every audit rather than presenting an unmatched line as a clean one.

Objections

What a billing director actually asks.

We already know the non-payable list. What does this add?

A blocklist tells you an item is non-payable. It does not tell you who eats it, and that is where the money is. List I is the patient's cost — a conversation at admission. Lists II, III and IV are yours: charges already deemed covered by the room, procedure or treatment rate, which you cannot bill to the insurer or to the patient. Those repeat on every claim carrying the same line, forever, until someone changes the billing master.

ClaimIQ models the bearer explicitly, which is why it can produce a hospital-write-off number at all.

Can I trust a number an AI produced?

The model does no arithmetic. Every rupee comes from a deterministic calculator working in exact decimal, and a verifier rejects any explanation containing a figure the calculator did not produce. The three buckets are checked to sum to the gross bill on every single audit.

The model's job is judgment — deciding which catalog rule an oddly-worded bill line matches — and even there it must cite the rule it decided from. An uncited deduction is discarded rather than shown.

What happens when it cannot read a line?

It says so. Lines it cannot identify are marked unassessed rather than reported as payable, they are excluded from the coverage figure, and if enough of the bill is unreadable the verdict becomes “could not fully check this claim” instead of a clean result.

“We found nothing” and “we could not look” are different answers and the screen distinguishes them.

Does patient data leave our network?

Not for native PDFs — those are parsed locally with no model call. For scans and photos, OCR runs on your machine but the resulting text is sent to whichever language-model provider you configure, and that text contains clinical detail. If that is unacceptable, run without a provider: auditing still works on deterministic rules, with the reduced recall stated above.

Is this HIPAA/IRDAI compliant, and can we deploy it hospital-wide?

Not as it stands, and the honest answer matters more than a reassuring one. There is no authentication, no per-user access control and no audit trail of who viewed which claim. It is a single-user local tool. A shared or hospital-wide deployment needs those three things built first.

The rule corpus is also an unverified snapshot that has not been checked against the current IRDAI circular, which the product discloses on every audit.

How much work is it to get an answer?

Upload the documents you already assemble for the TPA. The room rate, stay length, dates and diagnosis are read out of the bill and discharge summary. You confirm what was read, fill anything the documents did not state, and run the audit. There is no data entry beyond the fields no document supplied — and missing ones block the audit rather than being quietly defaulted.
